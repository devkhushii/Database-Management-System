# Part 9: Database Recovery, Logging & Durability
## Module 6: Checkpoints

> **Recap:** Module 5 gave us Undo/Redo Logging as the industry-standard technique. But there's a glaring problem we've been quietly ignoring: if the log just keeps growing forever, and recovery must scan the *entire* log from the very beginning of time, recovery after a crash on a database that's been running for three years would take... three years' worth of log replay. Obviously, that's not what happens. **Checkpoints** are the answer, and this module explains exactly how.

---

## 📍 The Problem Checkpoints Solve

Picture a database that's been running continuously for 400 days. Under Undo/Redo Logging (Module 5), every single write since day one generated a log record. If a crash happens on day 401, does recovery really need to replay 400 days of log records to figure out what to redo and undo?

Obviously not — but *why* not, and how does the DBMS know where it's safe to stop looking backward? That's exactly what checkpoints answer.

---

## 6.1 What is a Checkpoint?

### Definition

A **Checkpoint** is a deliberate, periodic operation performed by the DBMS during normal operation that records a snapshot of the system's state — enough information that recovery, after a future crash, only needs to examine the log **from the checkpoint forward**, not from the beginning of time.

### Real-Life Analogy

Think of a video game with a save-point system. You don't have to replay the entire game from the very first level every time you die — you resume from your **last saved checkpoint**. The game doesn't need to remember every single button you pressed since you started playing; it only needs to remember enough state (your position, inventory, health) at the checkpoint, plus whatever happened since then.

A database checkpoint works the same way: it's a periodically saved "resume point" that lets recovery skip over vast stretches of ancient, already-settled history.

### Why Checkpoints Exist

Directly connecting to Module 1's Recovery Goal of **"Minimize Recovery Time"**: without checkpoints, recovery time would grow unboundedly with the age and activity level of the database, since the entire log since inception would need scanning. Checkpoints impose a hard, predictable **ceiling** on how far back recovery ever needs to look — typically just since the last checkpoint, which administrators can schedule to happen every few minutes.

### What a Checkpoint Actually Records

At minimum, a checkpoint typically captures:

- **A special checkpoint log record**, marking the checkpoint's position (LSN) in the log.
- **The list of active (in-progress) transactions** at the moment of the checkpoint — this becomes the seed for the Analysis phase in Module 7.
- **The list of dirty pages** currently in the buffer pool at that moment (in more sophisticated implementations, like ARIES's fuzzy checkpoint — Section 6.4) — this tells recovery exactly which pages might still need redoing.

---

## 6.2 Why Checkpoints Exist — Deeper Dive

Beyond just bounding recovery time, checkpoints serve several closely related purposes:

1. **Bounding recovery time:** As explained above — the primary motivation.
2. **Allowing log truncation/reclamation:** Once a checkpoint confirms that everything before a certain LSN is no longer needed for crash recovery, that portion of the log can potentially be archived or discarded (subject to other needs like replication or PITR, covered in Module 9) — freeing disk space.
3. **Providing a natural point to flush dirty pages:** Checkpoints often coincide with (or trigger) flushing some dirty buffer pool pages to disk, which itself reduces the amount of redo work needed later.

---

## 6.3 Sharp Checkpoints

### What is a Sharp Checkpoint?

A **Sharp Checkpoint** (also called a **consistent checkpoint**) is the simplest, most conceptually straightforward checkpoint strategy: at the moment of checkpointing, the DBMS **pauses all transaction activity**, flushes **every single dirty page** in the buffer pool to disk, writes a checkpoint record to the log, and only then resumes normal transaction processing.

### Visual: Sharp Checkpoint Timeline

```
Normal operation ──────┬──────────────────────────┬──────► Normal operation resumes
                        │                            │
                        ▼                            ▼
              ┌───────────────────────────┐
              │  ⏸  ALL transactions PAUSED  │
              │  Flush EVERY dirty page       │
              │  Write checkpoint record        │
              │  ▶  Transactions RESUME          │
              └───────────────────────────┘
                     (This pause can be lengthy!)
```

### Why It's Called "Sharp"

Because the resulting on-disk state is perfectly, immediately, unambiguously consistent — a "sharp," clean line is drawn: everything before the checkpoint is guaranteed durable, no exceptions, no partial state.

### Advantages
- Extremely simple to reason about and implement.
- Recovery after a crash is trivial: everything before the checkpoint is guaranteed correctly on disk; only log records *after* the checkpoint need to be considered at all.

### Disadvantages
- **Stops the world:** All transaction processing halts during the checkpoint — completely unacceptable for any system with real-time or high-availability requirements, since it directly causes a visible service pause.
- Flushing *every* dirty page, even ones only recently touched by long-running transactions, can be a large, bursty I/O spike.

### When Sharp Checkpoints Are Actually Used

Given the severe downside, sharp checkpoints are mostly a **teaching tool** and are occasionally used in specific niche systems where brief full pauses are tolerable (e.g., certain embedded or single-user databases with light workloads) — production, highly concurrent OLTP engines like PostgreSQL and InnoDB use the far more sophisticated **Fuzzy Checkpoint** approach instead.

---

## 6.4 Fuzzy Checkpoints

### What is a Fuzzy Checkpoint?

A **Fuzzy Checkpoint** is the checkpoint strategy actually used by virtually all production databases (and formalized as part of ARIES, which we cover fully in Module 7). Unlike a sharp checkpoint, a fuzzy checkpoint does **not** pause transaction processing and does **not** require flushing all dirty pages immediately.

### How It Works

1. The DBMS writes a **checkpoint-begin record** to the log.
2. It records the **current state**: the list of active transactions (the **Transaction Table**) and the list of currently dirty pages (the **Dirty Page Table**) — both concepts we'll formalize fully in Module 7.
3. It writes a **checkpoint-end record** containing this captured information.
4. Transaction processing **continues throughout this entire process, uninterrupted** — new transactions can begin, existing ones can commit, and pages can become dirty, all while the checkpoint record is being assembled and written.
5. Crucially, dirty pages are **not** force-flushed as part of the checkpoint itself — they continue to be flushed lazily by the buffer manager's normal background activity, exactly as usual.

### Why It's Called "Fuzzy"

Because the checkpoint doesn't represent one single, perfectly clean instant in time — it's a bit "blurry" or "fuzzy," spanning the period during which the checkpoint information was being gathered, with ongoing concurrent activity. Recovery has to be smart enough to account for this fuzziness (which is exactly what ARIES's Analysis phase does — previewed here, detailed fully in Module 7).

### Visual: Fuzzy Checkpoint Timeline

```
Normal operation continues, uninterrupted, throughout:
   ...txn writes... ...txn writes... ...txn commits...
        │
        ▼
   [Checkpoint-Begin record written]
        │           ...txn writes continue in parallel...
        ▼
   [Snapshot: Transaction Table + Dirty Page Table captured]
        │           ...more txn activity continues in parallel...
        ▼
   [Checkpoint-End record written]
        │
        ▼
   Normal operation continues — NO pause occurred anywhere in this process
```

### Advantages
- **No service interruption:** Transactions never pause — a critical requirement for any serious production OLTP system.
- **Smooth I/O:** No sudden burst of forced flushes; the buffer manager continues its normal, gradual flushing pace.

### Disadvantages
- **More complex recovery logic:** Because the checkpoint doesn't represent one clean instant, the Analysis phase must carefully reconcile the checkpoint's captured Transaction/Dirty Page Tables with whatever additional log activity occurred during and after the checkpoint process — this complexity is exactly why ARIES exists as a formal, rigorous algorithm (Module 7).
- Slightly larger recovery scan window than a theoretical sharp checkpoint would produce, since recovery must look slightly further back to account for the "fuzziness."

---

## 6.5 Incremental Checkpoints

### What is an Incremental Checkpoint?

An **Incremental Checkpoint** is a refinement on the fuzzy checkpoint idea, where instead of taking one large periodic checkpoint, the DBMS continuously and gradually flushes dirty pages in small increments over time — often driven by tracking the **oldest dirty page's LSN** in the buffer pool — such that the "distance" recovery would need to replay is kept continuously small, rather than growing and then sharply resetting at each discrete checkpoint event.

### Why It Exists

Even fuzzy checkpoints, if scheduled too infrequently, can let a large backlog of dirty pages accumulate between checkpoints, meaning recovery still has a potentially large window to redo. Incremental checkpointing smooths this out further: rather than a periodic "big" checkpoint event, the system continuously nudges dirty pages toward disk in the background, keeping the recovery window consistently short at all times, not just right after a checkpoint completes.

### How It Relates to the Dirty Page Table

Recall from fuzzy checkpoints: the Dirty Page Table records, for each dirty page, the LSN of the log record that **first** made it dirty (often called the **RecLSN**, or recovery LSN, for that page). Incremental checkpointing uses this information actively: it targets flushing the pages with the **oldest RecLSN** first, since those are the ones that would force recovery to scan furthest back in the log if a crash happened right now.

### Visual: Incremental vs Periodic Checkpoint Behavior

```
PERIODIC (FUZZY) CHECKPOINTS:
Recovery window (LSN distance) over time:

  large │      ╱│           ╱│           ╱│
        │    ╱  │         ╱  │         ╱  │
  small │  ╱    │       ╱    │       ╱    │
        └──────checkpoint──────checkpoint──────► time
        (grows between checkpoints, resets sharply at each one)


INCREMENTAL CHECKPOINTING:
Recovery window (LSN distance) over time:

  large │
        │
  small │ ─────────────────────────────────────► time
        (kept continuously small via gradual background flushing)
```

### Advantages
- Even smoother I/O pattern than periodic fuzzy checkpoints — no bursts at all, just steady, continuous background work.
- Consistently short, predictable recovery windows at essentially any point in time, not just right after a checkpoint.

### Disadvantages
- More continuous background I/O overhead during normal operation — a constant "hum" of flush activity rather than periodic, more easily scheduled bursts.
- Slightly more complex bookkeeping to continuously track and prioritize the oldest dirty pages.

---

## 6.6 Checkpoint Scheduling

### The Core Trade-off

Checkpoint scheduling is a direct, textbook trade-off:

```
   MORE FREQUENT CHECKPOINTS                LESS FREQUENT CHECKPOINTS
   ─────────────────────────                ──────────────────────────
   ✅ Shorter recovery time after crash       ❌ Longer recovery time after crash
   ❌ More overhead during normal operation    ✅ Less overhead during normal operation
   ❌ More frequent I/O bursts (if using        ✅ Fewer I/O bursts
      fuzzy, not incremental)
```

### Common Scheduling Strategies

- **Time-based:** Checkpoint every N minutes (e.g., PostgreSQL's default `checkpoint_timeout`, typically 5 minutes).
- **Log-volume-based:** Checkpoint after every N megabytes of new log data has been generated (e.g., PostgreSQL's `max_wal_size`, which triggers a checkpoint if WAL grows too large before the time-based trigger fires).
- **Activity-based:** Some systems trigger checkpoints based on the number of dirty pages or transaction volume, rather than pure wall-clock time.
- **Manual/explicit:** DBAs can often force an immediate checkpoint (e.g., `CHECKPOINT;` in PostgreSQL) before planned maintenance, upgrades, or backups.

### PostgreSQL's Two Checkpoint Triggers (Named Example)

PostgreSQL specifically triggers a checkpoint whenever **either** of two conditions is met first:

1. `checkpoint_timeout` has elapsed since the last checkpoint (time-based), or
2. WAL generation has reached `max_wal_size` since the last checkpoint (volume-based).

This dual-trigger approach ensures checkpoints happen frequently enough during bursts of heavy write activity (bounded by volume) while also happening during quiet periods (bounded by time), rather than relying on just one dimension.

## 6.7 Checkpoint Recovery

### How Checkpoints Are Actually Used During Recovery

This section is the payoff for everything above — connecting checkpoints directly back to the recovery workflow from Module 1 and previewing Module 7's ARIES algorithm in concrete terms:

1. On restart after a crash, the DBMS locates the **most recent completed checkpoint** record in the log.
2. It loads that checkpoint's saved **Transaction Table** and **Dirty Page Table** as its starting point — instead of starting with empty tables and scanning from the dawn of the log.
3. The **Analysis phase** (Module 7) then scans forward from the checkpoint's LSN — not from LSN 1 — updating these tables based on whatever log activity occurred between the checkpoint and the crash.
4. This dramatically bounded scan produces the final, accurate lists of "who needs redo" and "who needs undo," and recovery proceeds exactly as described in Module 5's Undo/Redo Logging algorithm, just scoped to a much smaller portion of the log.

### Visual: Checkpoint-Bounded Recovery Scan

```
LSN:     1 ──────────────────────► 5000 ─────► 5200 (checkpoint) ─────► 5450 (CRASH)
                                                    │
        Without checkpoint:                        │
        recovery would scan ALL of this ◄───────────┘
        [1 ─────────────────────────────────────────► 5450]   ❌ SLOW

        With checkpoint:
        recovery only scans this  ◄──────────────────┘
                              [5200 ──────────► 5450]           ✅ FAST
```

## 6.8 Checkpoint Algorithm — Fuzzy Checkpoint, Step by Step

To make this fully concrete, here is the fuzzy checkpoint algorithm as commonly implemented:

```
ALGORITHM: Fuzzy Checkpoint

1. Write log record: [BEGIN_CHECKPOINT]

2. Capture Transaction Table (TT):
   For every currently active transaction:
      record { TxnID, Status, LastLSN }

3. Capture Dirty Page Table (DPT):
   For every currently dirty page in the buffer pool:
      record { PageID, RecLSN }   // RecLSN = LSN that first dirtied this page

4. Write log record: [END_CHECKPOINT, TT, DPT]

5. Update a well-known, fixed on-disk location (e.g., PostgreSQL's
   pg_control file, or a "master record") to point to the LSN of this
   checkpoint — so future recovery attempts know where to start looking
   WITHOUT having to scan the entire log to find the last checkpoint.

6. Transaction processing was NEVER paused during any of the above steps.
```

> 💡 **Interview Insight:** Step 5 is very frequently overlooked by candidates but is a favorite deep-dive question: *"How does recovery even find the last checkpoint quickly, without scanning the whole log?"* The answer: a small, fixed, well-known on-disk pointer (like PostgreSQL's control file) is updated at the end of each checkpoint, giving recovery an O(1) way to jump straight to the right starting point.

## 6.9 PostgreSQL vs MySQL (InnoDB) vs Oracle — Checkpoint Behavior

| Aspect | PostgreSQL | InnoDB (MySQL) | Oracle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Checkpoint type | Fuzzy | Fuzzy (with continuous flushing, close to incremental in spirit) | Incremental-style, driven by the DBWn background process |
| Time trigger | `checkpoint_timeout` | Innodb continuously flushes based on `innodb_max_dirty_pages_pct` and redo log capacity | Based on redo log switches and `FAST_START_MTTR_TARGET` |
| Volume trigger | `max_wal_size` | Redo log capacity threshold | Redo log group size |
| Fixed pointer location | `pg_control` file | Redo log header | Control file |
| Manual trigger | `CHECKPOINT;` command | `FLUSH TABLES ... FOR EXPORT` (partial), or via engine internals | `ALTER SYSTEM CHECKPOINT;` |

> Deeper engine-specific configuration guidance continues in Module 11 — the point here is to recognize that despite different names and knobs, all three engines are solving the exact scheduling trade-off described in Section 6.6.

## 6.10 Advantages of Checkpointing (Overall)

- Dramatically bounds and predicts recovery time — a direct, measurable improvement to a system's RTO (Recovery Time Objective, formally introduced in Module 10).
- Enables safe log truncation/archival, preventing unbounded log growth (Module 3's storage overhead concern, Module 9's archival practices).
- Fuzzy and incremental variants add this benefit with **zero or near-zero disruption** to live transaction processing.

## 6.11 Disadvantages / Costs of Checkpointing

- Consumes I/O and CPU resources during normal operation — a direct tax on throughput, however small, that must be tuned appropriately.
- Poorly tuned checkpoint frequency (too rare) defeats the purpose (long recovery); too frequent wastes resources unnecessarily — this is a genuine, ongoing tuning responsibility for DBAs (fully explored with concrete guidance in Module 12).

## 6.12 Best Practices

- Tune checkpoint frequency based on your actual RTO requirements — a system where 10 minutes of recovery time is perfectly acceptable doesn't need aggressive, resource-hungry checkpointing.
- Monitor checkpoint duration and frequency in production — checkpoints that take longer than the interval between them indicate a serious capacity problem.
- Prefer engines/configurations using fuzzy or incremental checkpointing for any system with real-time availability requirements — never rely on sharp checkpoint behavior in a live production OLTP system.

## 6.13 Common Mistakes

- Assuming more frequent checkpoints are always better — this ignores the very real overhead cost explored in Section 6.6.
- Forgetting that a checkpoint does **not**, by itself, mean "everything is now safely on disk" under fuzzy checkpointing — dirty pages recorded in the Dirty Page Table might still be pending flush, which is exactly why Analysis (Module 7) must still process them.
- Confusing a checkpoint with a **backup** — a checkpoint is purely an internal recovery-time optimization; it is not a substitute for the backup and restore strategies covered in Module 9.

## 6.14 Edge Cases

- **Checkpoint during a crash:** What if the system crashes literally *during* a checkpoint's writing process? Because fuzzy checkpoints use a `BEGIN_CHECKPOINT`/`END_CHECKPOINT` pair, recovery can detect an incomplete checkpoint (missing `END_CHECKPOINT`) and simply fall back to the *previous* completed checkpoint instead — a clean, well-defined edge case, not a special failure mode.
- **Extremely long-running transactions spanning many checkpoints:** Such a transaction remains in the Transaction Table across multiple checkpoint cycles, and its RecLSN in the Dirty Page Table may be very old — this can prevent log truncation from progressing very far, a genuine, frequently-seen production issue caused by long-running transactions (sometimes called "log bloat due to idle transactions").

---

## 6.15 Chapter Summary

- **Checkpoints** bound recovery time by giving the DBMS a "resume point" so recovery doesn't need to scan the log from the very beginning.
- **Sharp Checkpoints** pause all activity and flush everything — simple but unacceptable for production due to service interruption.
- **Fuzzy Checkpoints** — the industry standard — capture the Transaction Table and Dirty Page Table without pausing transaction processing, at the cost of more complex recovery logic (formalized fully by ARIES in Module 7).
- **Incremental Checkpoints** refine this further by continuously flushing dirty pages in the background, keeping the recovery window consistently small rather than sawtoothing between checkpoints.
- **Checkpoint Scheduling** balances recovery-time benefits against normal-operation overhead, typically using time-based and volume-based triggers together.
- A small, fixed, well-known on-disk pointer lets recovery instantly locate the most recent checkpoint without scanning the log.

## 6.16 Cheat Sheet

| Term | One-Line Meaning |
|---|---|
| Checkpoint | A resume point bounding how far back recovery scans |
| Sharp Checkpoint | Pauses everything, flushes all dirty pages — simple, disruptive |
| Fuzzy Checkpoint | No pause; captures TT + DPT snapshot — industry standard |
| Incremental Checkpoint | Continuous background flushing, keeps recovery window small |
| Transaction Table (TT) | Active transactions at checkpoint time |
| Dirty Page Table (DPT) | Dirty pages + their RecLSN at checkpoint time |
| RecLSN | LSN that first dirtied a given page |
| Checkpoint-Begin/End | Bracket records marking a complete, valid checkpoint |
