## 13.1 Banking

### Business Context

Core banking systems (account balances, ledgers, transaction records) are the textbook example of a system where correctness is non-negotiable — even a tiny, rare data-loss event can mean real money silently vanishing or duplicating, with direct regulatory and legal consequences.

### Dominant Constraint

**RPO ≈ 0.** Losing even a single committed transaction (Module 10) is unacceptable — banking regulations in most jurisdictions mandate this explicitly.

### Recovery Strategy Design

- **Durability setting:** Strict, fully synchronous commit (`synchronous_commit=on` / `innodb_flush_log_at_trx_commit=1`, Module 4) — no relaxation whatsoever.
- **Buffer policy:** STEAL + NO-FORCE (Module 8) — standard, since ARIES-based recovery already guarantees correctness; there's no need for the performance-crippling NO-STEAL+FORCE combination.
- **Checkpointing:** Frequent fuzzy checkpoints (Module 6) tuned to keep recovery time low, since even brief unavailability during trading/banking hours has real financial cost.
- **Backup strategy:** Full backups plus continuous WAL/redo archiving enabling PITR (Module 9) — critical for the Human Error failure category (Module 2), since banking systems are also subject to operator mistakes.
- **Geo-redundancy:** Synchronous multi-region replication (Module 10) for the most critical ledger data, accepting the added commit latency as a mandatory cost of doing business.

### Failure Simulation Walkthrough

**Scenario:** A data center housing the primary banking database loses power permanently (Catastrophic Failure, Module 2, Section 2.10) at 2:15 PM, mid-business-day.

1. Automated health checks detect the primary's unavailability within seconds.
2. Because synchronous geo-replication was in place, the standby in a separate region has **zero data loss** — every committed transaction up to 2:15 PM is present.
3. Automatic (or rapidly executed manual, depending on the institution's risk tolerance for automated failover) failover promotes the standby (Module 10, Section 10.6).
4. Application traffic is redirected; service resumes within the institution's RTO target — often just minutes for tier-1 banking infrastructure.
5. Once the original data center's power is restored and infrastructure repaired, a carefully planned **failback** (Module 10, Section 10.7) re-synchronizes and eventually restores the original site's role, during a low-traffic window.

## 13.2 Payment Systems

### Business Context

Payment processors (credit card networks, digital wallets) must handle extremely high transaction volume with strict correctness guarantees — a duplicated or lost payment transaction directly translates to disputed charges, fraud exposure, or lost revenue.

### Dominant Constraint

**Both RPO ≈ 0 AND very low RTO** — unlike some banking back-office systems that could tolerate a few minutes of downtime, payment systems often need to keep processing transactions essentially continuously, since merchants and consumers expect real-time payment authorization around the clock.

### Recovery Strategy Design

- Similar durability strictness to banking (Section 13.1), but with even heavier investment in **High Availability** (Module 10, Section 10.4) — redundant servers within each region handling automatic, sub-second failover for routine component failures, layered *underneath* the geo-redundant DR architecture for catastrophic events.
- **Idempotency at the application layer** (a concept connecting back to Module 3 and Module 7's recovery idempotency, but applied to the payment API itself): payment APIs are typically designed so that a retried request (e.g., due to a client-side timeout during a genuine but unconfirmed transaction) doesn't result in a duplicate charge — directly mirroring the database-level idempotency lessons from CLRs (Module 3, Section 3.8).

### Failure Simulation Walkthrough

**Scenario:** A single database server (not the whole region) crashes (System Crash, Module 2, Section 2.2) during peak holiday shopping traffic.

1. HA infrastructure (Module 10, Section 10.4) detects the crash within seconds via health checks.
2. A local standby/replica within the same region is promoted almost instantly — this is HA in action, not full DR, since the failure is contained to a single server, not an entire region.
3. Meanwhile, standard ARIES-based crash recovery (Module 7) proceeds on the crashed server in the background, so it can rejoin the cluster as a healthy standby once fully recovered.
4. Total user-visible disruption: seconds, not minutes — a direct product of layering HA underneath DR rather than relying on cross-region failover for every single-server issue.

## 13.3 Airline Reservation

### Business Context

Airline reservation systems manage seat inventory, booking records, and pricing across a globally distributed customer base, with extremely high read concurrency and moderate write concurrency (many people searching flights; fewer, but still substantial, actual bookings).

### Dominant Constraint

**Consistency under massive concurrency** (a direct callback to Part 6's concurrency control) combined with a moderate RPO/RTO — losing a few seconds of the most recent bookings during a rare disaster is generally more tolerable than in banking, but double-booking the same seat (a consistency failure, not strictly a recovery failure, but closely related) is a serious, visible problem.

### Recovery Strategy Design

- Standard STEAL + NO-FORCE with ARIES (Modules 5, 7, 8) — no unusual durability requirements beyond typical production defaults.
- Strong emphasis on **process failure handling** (Module 2, Section 2.3) — with an enormous number of concurrent client connections (millions of simultaneous search/booking sessions globally), gracefully handling individual dropped connections without impacting other users is operationally critical.
- Regional read replicas (an HA/performance pattern, connecting to Module 10's geo-redundancy concepts but applied primarily for read scaling rather than pure DR) serving search queries from geographically nearby locations, with writes (actual bookings) routed to a primary system with appropriate durability guarantees.

### Failure Simulation Walkthrough

**Scenario:** A network partition (Module 2, Section 2.6) temporarily isolates one regional data center from the rest of the airline's global infrastructure.

1. Users in the affected region may experience degraded search performance (falling back to a more distant regional replica) but booking operations — routed to the still-reachable primary — continue functioning correctly.
2. Once network connectivity is restored, the previously isolated regional replica resynchronizes, catching up on any bookings/changes it missed.
3. Careful design (avoiding any local write acceptance during the partition, to prevent split-brain — Module 10, Section 10.15's edge case) ensures no double-booking occurred during the disruption.

## 13.4 Healthcare

### Business Context

Electronic Health Records (EHR) and hospital systems manage patient data where availability can be a matter of life and death (a clinician needing immediate access to allergy information or medication history), and where data integrity/audit trails are subject to strict regulatory compliance (e.g., HIPAA in the United States).

### Dominant Constraint

**High availability combined with long-term data retention and auditability** — healthcare records often must be retained and recoverable for years or decades, and every access/change must be auditable, connecting recovery strategy to compliance requirements beyond pure technical durability.

### Recovery Strategy Design

- HA architecture (Module 10) ensuring clinical systems remain available even during routine maintenance or component failures — downtime during an active emergency has severe real-world consequences.
- Robust PITR capability (Module 9) specifically to recover from human error (Module 2, Section 2.9) — healthcare data entry mistakes (wrong patient record modified, accidental deletion) are a realistic, recurring risk given the sheer volume of manual data entry in clinical settings.
- Long-term backup retention policies, often driven by regulatory requirements rather than pure technical RPO/RTO calculations — a direct example of Module 10's "business continuity" concept extending beyond pure disaster response into compliance obligations.
- Encryption of backups at rest and in transit (a security consideration layered on top of, but distinct from, the pure recovery mechanics covered in this handbook).

### Failure Simulation Walkthrough

**Scenario:** A hospital IT administrator accidentally runs an update script against the production EHR database instead of the staging environment, corrupting several thousand patient records (Human Error, Module 2, Section 2.9).

1. The mistake is discovered within an hour, when clinicians report incorrect data appearing for affected patients.
2. Using PITR (Module 9, Section 9.7), the database is restored to a point-in-time snapshot just before the erroneous script ran.
3. Because continuous log archiving was in place, minimal legitimate data (any correct changes made in the brief window between the mistake and its detection) needs to be manually reconciled — a real, sometimes painful trade-off in human-error recovery scenarios, underscoring why prevention (access controls, staging/production separation) remains the best defense, exactly as noted back in Module 2's best practices.

## 13.5 Cloud Databases

### Business Context

Managed cloud database services (Amazon RDS, Google Cloud SQL, Azure SQL Database, and similar) serve an enormously diverse customer base with wildly varying RPO/RTO needs, from hobbyist projects to mission-critical enterprise workloads — all on shared underlying infrastructure.

### Dominant Constraint

**Automation and self-service configurability at massive scale** — the recovery strategy itself must be largely automated and exposed as simple, configurable options to customers who may not have deep database recovery expertise (directly connecting to why this entire handbook exists — most engineers using these services benefit enormously from understanding what's happening "under the hood").

### Recovery Strategy Design

- Automated, continuous backup and PITR offered as a near-default feature (Module 9), typically with configurable retention windows.
- Multi-AZ deployment options (Module 10, Section 10.5) offered as a straightforward toggle, abstracting away the underlying synchronous replication complexity from the customer.
- Automated crash recovery (Modules 3–8) handled entirely transparently — customers of managed cloud databases rarely, if ever, need to think about WAL or ARIES directly, precisely because the cloud provider has implemented it correctly on their behalf.
- Clear separation of responsibility: the cloud provider handles crash recovery and infrastructure-level DR automatically, while **human error and application-level mistakes remain the customer's responsibility** — a crucial, frequently misunderstood point directly echoing Module 2's industry usage notes.

### Failure Simulation Walkthrough

**Scenario:** A customer using a managed cloud database service accidentally deletes a critical table via a misconfigured migration script.

1. The cloud provider's underlying crash-recovery and HA mechanisms are entirely irrelevant here — the delete was a legitimate, committed operation, not a crash.
2. The customer must use the cloud provider's PITR feature (essentially Module 9's PITR, exposed through a managed UI/API) to restore to a point in time just before the mistaken deletion — typically self-service, without needing to contact the provider's support team.
3. This scenario is a clean, real-world illustration of exactly the Module 2 point: **infrastructure automation protects against infrastructure failures, not against committed human mistakes** — that boundary doesn't disappear just because the database is "in the cloud."

## 13.6 E-commerce

### Business Context

E-commerce platforms manage product catalogs, inventory, orders, and payment integration, with extreme traffic spikes during sales events (e.g., major shopping holidays) and a strong business sensitivity to both downtime (lost sales) and data loss (incorrect inventory/order records).

### Dominant Constraint

**Handling extreme, bursty write load** (order placement during flash sales) while maintaining reasonable RPO/RTO — not as strict as banking, but downtime during a major sales event has severe, direct, and highly visible revenue impact.

### Recovery Strategy Design

- Aggressive **Group Commit** tuning (Module 12, Section 12.2) to handle burst concurrency efficiently during traffic spikes.
- Checkpoint scheduling (Module 6, Module 12) tuned to avoid checkpoint-induced I/O bursts coinciding with anticipated peak traffic windows (a practical, real-world application of the checkpoint-timing trade-offs discussed throughout the handbook).
- Read replicas absorbing the enormous read volume from product browsing, isolating the primary's capacity for the write-heavy order-placement path.
- Robust backup and PITR (Module 9) to recover from inventory data corruption incidents, which — given the scale and automation of e-commerce systems — are a realistic operational risk (e.g., a buggy inventory-sync job).

### Failure Simulation Walkthrough

**Scenario:** During a major flash sale, write throughput spikes to 10x normal levels, and the database's checkpoint process begins falling behind, causing WAL/redo log to grow rapidly (a direct, practical instance of Module 6's checkpoint scheduling trade-off, and Module 12's recovery time discussion).

1. Monitoring alerts on rapid log growth and increasing checkpoint duration.
2. Engineers temporarily adjust checkpoint frequency/target settings (Module 6, Module 12) to better cope with the burst, accepting a slightly higher live I/O overhead in exchange for preventing unbounded log growth.
3. Post-event, the team reviews the incident and permanently adjusts capacity planning (more headroom on log storage, pre-emptive checkpoint tuning ahead of known future sale events) — directly reflecting Module 1's framing of recovery as an ongoing engineering discipline, not a "set once and forget" configuration.

## 13.7 Financial Trading

### Business Context

Financial trading platforms (stock exchanges, algorithmic trading systems) require extremely low latency for every operation, since trading decisions can be time-sensitive to microseconds, while simultaneously requiring extremely strong correctness guarantees, since incorrect trade records have direct, immediate financial and regulatory consequences.

### Dominant Constraint

**Simultaneously minimal latency AND zero data loss** — arguably the single hardest combination of requirements covered across all these case studies, since the two goals often directly conflict (Module 4's commit latency discussion, Module 10's synchronous replication latency discussion).

### Recovery Strategy Design

- Extremely careful log buffer and Group Commit tuning (Module 12) to minimize the latency cost of durability as much as physically possible, often involving specialized, high-performance storage hardware specifically for the log/redo path.
- Synchronous, but topologically optimized, geo-replication (Module 10) — sometimes using specially selected, low-latency-distance secondary sites rather than maximally distant ones, deliberately trading some catastrophic-failure protection for reduced replication latency (a genuine, explicit business risk trade-off, not an oversight).
- Extremely aggressive monitoring and automated failover (Module 10, Section 10.6) with sub-second detection and switchover targets.

### Failure Simulation Walkthrough

**Scenario:** A trading platform's primary database experiences a sudden hardware failure (Module 2, Section 2.8) during active market hours.

1. Given the synchronous replication architecture, the standby has zero data loss for all committed trades up to the failure instant.
2. Automated failover, engineered and tested extensively in advance (Module 10, Section 10.3's DR drills, taken to an extreme level of rigor given the stakes), completes in well under a second in well-architected systems.
3. In-flight, uncommitted trade requests at the moment of failure are handled by the client-side/application-layer retry and idempotency logic (echoing Section 13.2's payment systems discussion) rather than the database recovery layer alone.

## 13.8 Enterprise Applications

### Business Context

Large enterprise applications (ERP systems, CRM platforms, internal business tools) typically have more relaxed real-time requirements than the previous case studies, but still require reliable, predictable recovery to avoid disrupting core business operations (payroll, supply chain, customer relationship data).

### Dominant Constraint

**Cost-effectiveness balanced against reasonable, business-appropriate RPO/RTO** — enterprise systems often serve internal, not public-facing, needs, meaning a few hours of downtime during a genuine disaster, while inconvenient, is typically survivable in a way that would be unacceptable for the payment or trading case studies above.

### Recovery Strategy Design

- Standard nightly full backups plus periodic incremental/differential backups (Module 9) — often sufficient given a more relaxed RPO (e.g., a few hours) compared to the near-zero requirements of banking or trading.
- Single-region HA (Module 10, Section 10.4) for routine component failures, with a documented but less aggressively automated DR plan for true catastrophic scenarios, reflecting the appropriately lower investment level for this risk profile.
- Regular, but not necessarily continuous, DR drills (Module 10, Section 10.3) — quarterly or semi-annual testing is often a reasonable, cost-effective cadence for this risk profile, compared to the near-constant validation rigor appropriate for financial trading systems.

### Failure Simulation Walkthrough

**Scenario:** An enterprise's on-premises data center suffers a multi-day outage due to a major regional infrastructure failure.

1. Given the documented DR plan (Module 10, Section 10.3), the operations team executes a **planned, manual failover** procedure (deliberately manual rather than automated, given the lower urgency and correspondingly lower investment in this system's DR automation) to a secondary, cloud-hosted environment.
2. The most recent nightly backup plus any available archived logs are used to restore the secondary environment (Module 9), accepting the documented, pre-agreed RPO (potentially a few hours of lost data, communicated clearly to business stakeholders as an accepted risk for this system tier).
3. Once the primary data center is restored, a carefully planned failback (Module 10, Section 10.7) occurs during a scheduled maintenance window.

## 13.10 Advantages of Studying Real Case Studies

- Demonstrates that every concept from Modules 1–12 is a **tool in a toolbox**, not a mandatory checklist — the skill being tested (and the skill worth developing) is choosing the right combination for a given business context.
- Prepares you directly for the most common senior-level interview question format: *"Design the recovery strategy for [industry/system type]."*
- Reinforces that RPO/RTO (Module 10) is genuinely the starting point for every real recovery architecture decision, not an afterthought.

## 13.11 Disadvantages / Limitations of Case-Study Learning

- Real systems are messier and more constrained by legacy infrastructure, budget, and organizational history than any clean case study can fully capture — these walkthroughs are illustrative starting points, not literal blueprints to copy verbatim.
- Regulatory and compliance requirements vary significantly by jurisdiction and are constantly evolving — always verify current, specific regulatory requirements for any real system in a regulated industry like banking or healthcare.

## 13.12 Best Practices

- When designing (or discussing, in an interview) a recovery strategy for any system, always start by explicitly asking: *what are the RPO and RTO requirements, and why?* — resist the urge to jump straight to specific technical mechanisms before establishing this foundation.
- Recognize that different systems within the *same* organization often warrant different recovery strategies — a payment-processing microservice and an internal analytics dashboard within the same company should not necessarily share the same recovery architecture.

## 13.13 Common Mistakes

- Applying a "maximum safety" recovery strategy uniformly across all systems regardless of actual business need — wasteful, and often counterproductive if it introduces unnecessary latency or complexity into systems that didn't need it.
- Underestimating the human error risk category (Module 2) in favor of over-indexing on infrastructure-level disaster scenarios — as seen in the Healthcare and Cloud Databases case studies, human error is frequently the more common, more immediately relevant risk.

## 13.14 Edge Cases

- Systems that genuinely need *different* recovery strategies for different portions of their own data (e.g., an e-commerce platform might need near-zero RPO for payment records but can tolerate a much more relaxed RPO for browsing/analytics data) — a sophisticated pattern worth recognizing, sometimes implemented via separate databases or tables with independently tuned recovery configurations.

## 13.15 Chapter Summary

- **Banking and Financial Trading** demand near-zero RPO/RTO, justifying expensive synchronous geo-replication and strict durability settings.
- **Payment Systems** layer HA underneath DR for fast, routine failure handling, combined with application-level idempotency.
- **Airline Reservation** prioritizes concurrency correctness and global read scaling alongside moderate durability needs.
- **Healthcare** emphasizes availability and PITR-driven human-error recovery, layered with long-term compliance-driven retention.
- **Cloud Databases** automate and productize recovery mechanisms for a broad customer base, while human error remains explicitly the customer's responsibility.
- **E-commerce** must handle extreme, bursty write load, tuning checkpoints and Group Commit specifically around anticipated peak events.
- **Enterprise Applications** typically accept more relaxed RPO/RTO, favoring cost-effective, standard backup strategies over expensive real-time redundancy.
- Across every case study, the same underlying toolkit (Modules 1–12) is applied differently based on each industry's specific, explicit RPO/RTO and risk tolerance.
