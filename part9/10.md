# Part 9: Database Recovery, Logging & Durability
## Module 10: Disaster Recovery

> **Recap:** Module 9 gave us the *tools* — full/incremental/differential backups, PITR, snapshots. This module gives us the *discipline* that wraps around those tools: how do you actually plan, measure, and organize a response to catastrophic failure? This is where database engineering meets business strategy — the concepts here (RPO, RTO) are numbers that executives, not just engineers, care deeply about.

---

## 📍 From Tools to Strategy

Module 9 answered "how do I take a backup and restore it?" This module answers a very different, harder question: **"How much data can we afford to lose, and how long can we afford to be down — and what do we build to meet those numbers?"** These aren't purely technical questions; they're business questions with technical answers, which is exactly why this module matters so much for senior-level interviews.

---

## 10.1 Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

### What is RPO?

**Recovery Point Objective (RPO)** is the maximum acceptable amount of **data loss**, measured in time, that a business can tolerate in the event of a failure. It answers: *"If disaster strikes right now, how far back in time can our restored data acceptably be?"*

### Real-Life Analogy

Imagine you save your essay draft every hour. If your laptop dies right before an hourly save, you lose, at most, one hour of work. Your **RPO is one hour** — you've decided that losing up to 60 minutes of work is an acceptable, tolerable risk, given the cost/effort of saving more frequently.

### How RPO Drives Backup Strategy

RPO is a direct, practical input into the backup design decisions from Module 9:

| Desired RPO | Backup Strategy Implication |
|---|---|
| 24 hours | Nightly full backup is sufficient |
| 1 hour | Hourly incremental/differential backups, or frequent log archiving |
| Near-zero (seconds) | Continuous backup / synchronous replication (Module 9, Section 9.8) |

### Formula/Framing

```
RPO = "How much data can we afford to lose?"
    = Time between the last successful backup/log-archive point
      and the moment of failure
```

### Real Example

A bank's core transaction ledger might have an RPO of **zero** — literally no committed transaction can ever be lost, requiring synchronous replication to a secondary site (Section 10.5). A personal blog's database might have an RPO of **24 hours** — losing a day's worth of unpublished draft edits is a minor inconvenience, not a crisis, so a nightly backup is entirely sufficient.

---

## 10.2 Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

### What is RTO?

**Recovery Time Objective (RTO)** is the maximum acceptable **duration of downtime** a business can tolerate after a failure occurs, before service must be restored. It answers: *"If disaster strikes right now, how long can we be down before it becomes unacceptable?"*

### Real-Life Analogy

If your essay-writing laptop dies, RPO tells you how much writing you lost (up to the last save). **RTO** tells you how long you can tolerate being unable to write at all before you *must* borrow a friend's laptop or buy a new one — even if that means the recovered essay isn't perfectly up to date.

### How RTO Drives Recovery Strategy

| Desired RTO | Recovery Strategy Implication |
|---|---|
| Hours to a day | Restore from backup (Module 9) is acceptable |
| Minutes | Hot standby replica with fast failover (Section 10.4) needed |
| Near-zero (seconds) | Fully automated failover, active-active architecture |

### RPO vs RTO — The Critical Distinction

This distinction is asked about constantly in interviews, and mixing them up is an instant red flag:

```
                    FAILURE OCCURS
                          │
   ◄────── RPO ──────────►│◄────────── RTO ──────────►
   (data loss window,      (downtime window,
    measured BACKWARD       measured FORWARD
    from failure)            from failure)

Last backup/          Failure           Service fully
sync point             happens           restored
    │                     │                    │
    ▼                     ▼                    ▼
────●─────────────────────●────────────────────●──────►  time
```

> 💡 **Interview Insight:** A clean, memorable way to state the difference: **RPO is about data you can afford to lose (backward-looking); RTO is about time you can afford to be down (forward-looking).** Confusing these two in an interview answer is one of the most common mistakes candidates make on this exact topic.

## 10.3 Disaster Recovery Planning

### What is a Disaster Recovery (DR) Plan?

A **Disaster Recovery Plan** is the documented, tested, organizational procedure for responding to a catastrophic failure (Module 2, Section 2.10) — covering not just the technical restore steps, but roles, responsibilities, communication procedures, and decision criteria.

### Key Components of a DR Plan

1. **Defined RPO and RTO targets** for each system, agreed upon with business stakeholders (not just decided unilaterally by engineering).
2. **Documented backup and replication architecture** meeting those targets (built from Module 9's tools and Section 10.4/10.5's HA/geo-redundancy concepts).
3. **Clear roles and responsibilities:** who declares a disaster, who executes the failover, who communicates with customers/stakeholders.
4. **Step-by-step runbooks** for executing failover/restore procedures — written clearly enough that someone other than the original author could follow them under pressure.
5. **Regular DR drills:** scheduled, realistic tests of the entire plan (not just the backup restore, but the full organizational response) — directly connecting to Module 9's backup verification principle, but at an organizational scale.
6. **Post-incident review process:** documenting what actually happened during any real disaster or drill, and updating the plan accordingly.

### Why DR Planning is Often Underinvested In

DR planning has a distinctive characteristic: it's expensive and time-consuming to build and maintain, but produces **zero visible business value** until the rare moment it's actually needed — at which point it becomes the single most valuable thing the organization has ever built. This asymmetry is precisely why many organizations under-invest in DR planning until a painful incident forces the issue — a pattern worth understanding for both engineering judgment and interview discussions about prioritization trade-offs.

## 10.4 High Availability vs Disaster Recovery

### The Critical Distinction

This is another pair of terms frequently confused, and worth pinning down precisely:

| | High Availability (HA) | Disaster Recovery (DR) |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Minimize downtime from *common*, smaller-scale failures | Recover from *rare*, large-scale/catastrophic failures |
| **Typical scope** | Single data center, redundant components within it | Cross-region/cross-site, protects against total site loss |
| **Example failures handled** | Single server crash, single disk failure | Data center fire, regional power grid failure, natural disaster |
| **Typical RTO** | Seconds (often automatic, no human intervention) | Minutes to hours (often requires deliberate failover decision) |
| **Typical mechanism** | Redundant servers, RAID, automatic failover within a cluster | Geo-replication, off-site backups, cross-region failover |

### Real-Life Analogy

**High Availability** is like a car having a spare tire in the trunk — if one tire blows out, you can be back on the road in minutes without needing a whole new car. **Disaster Recovery** is like having a completely separate backup car parked at a friend's house across town — if your entire car is totaled in an accident, you have a fallback, but getting to it and getting it running takes considerably longer.

### Why Both Are Needed Together

A robust system needs **both** — HA handles the frequent, smaller incidents smoothly and often invisibly to users, while DR provides the safety net for the rare, severe incidents that HA alone cannot address (because HA's redundancy typically lives within a single site, which a genuine catastrophe could destroy entirely).

## 10.5 Geo-Redundancy

### What is Geo-Redundancy?

**Geo-Redundancy** is the practice of maintaining copies of data and/or fully operational infrastructure in **physically separate geographic regions**, specifically to survive a disaster that destroys or disables an entire region (the Catastrophic Failure category from Module 2, Section 2.10).

### Why It's Necessary (Direct Callback to Module 2's Core DR Principle)

Recall the fire analogy from Module 2: a backup stored in the same building as the primary system is destroyed alongside it. Geo-redundancy is the formal architectural pattern that guarantees this never happens — by design, no single regional disaster (earthquake, flood, regional power grid failure, large-scale outage) can take out both the primary system and its geo-redundant copy simultaneously.

### Levels of Geo-Redundancy

```
Least redundant ──────────────────────────────► Most redundant

Single data center    Multi-AZ (same region,     Multi-region
(no geo-redundancy)   different buildings)         (different
                                                     continents/regions)
```

- **Multi-AZ (Availability Zone):** Data replicated across different physical buildings/power sources within the same broad region — protects against building-level or single-facility disasters, but not truly regional disasters (e.g., a region-wide natural disaster or grid failure could still affect all AZs in that region, though this is rare by design).
- **Multi-Region:** Data replicated across genuinely distant geographic regions — protects against essentially any single-region disaster, at the cost of higher replication latency (physics: light, and therefore network signals, takes measurably longer to travel further).

### Trade-off: Synchronous vs Asynchronous Geo-Replication

| Approach | RPO | Performance Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Synchronous | Near-zero (every commit waits for confirmation from the remote region) | High latency added to every single commit — often impractical across truly distant regions due to speed-of-light network delay |
| Asynchronous | Small but non-zero (remote region lags slightly behind primary) | Minimal latency impact on the primary's commit performance |

This is a direct, practical extension of Module 4's WAL commit protocol discussion — synchronous geo-replication essentially extends the "must be durable before commit is acknowledged" rule all the way out to a remote data center, at a real, sometimes substantial latency cost.

## 10.6 Failover

### What is Failover?

**Failover** is the process of switching operations from a failed (or failing) primary system to a standby/replica system, so that service can continue with minimal disruption.

### Types of Failover

- **Automatic Failover:** The system itself detects the primary's failure and switches to the standby without human intervention — necessary for very low RTO targets, but requires careful design to avoid false positives (mistakenly failing over when the primary was actually fine, just briefly slow or network-partitioned — a genuine, tricky distributed-systems problem previewed here and explored fully in Part 10).
- **Manual Failover:** A human operator makes the decision and executes the switch — slower, but avoids the risk of an automated system making a wrong call during an ambiguous situation (like a network partition that looks like a failure but isn't).

### Failover Process (Simplified)

```
1. Failure detected (automatically via health checks,
   or manually reported/observed)
2. Decision made: is failover actually warranted?
   (Critical step — avoiding "split-brain," previewed
   in Module 2's network failure discussion, and covered
   fully in Part 10)
3. Standby is promoted to become the new primary
4. Application traffic is redirected (DNS change, load
   balancer reconfiguration, connection string update, etc.)
5. Original primary, once repaired, does NOT automatically
   resume as primary — see Failback, next section
```

## 10.7 Failback

### What is Failback?

**Failback** is the reverse process: once the original primary system has been repaired and is healthy again, deliberately switching operations *back* to it from the standby that took over during failover.

### Why Failback is Its Own Deliberate Step (Not Automatic)

This is a commonly overlooked but important nuance: failback is **not** simply "undo the failover." By the time the original primary is repaired, the standby has likely processed additional transactions that the original primary never saw. Failback requires:

1. **Re-synchronizing** the repaired original primary with all the changes that occurred on the standby while it was serving as primary.
2. Choosing a **safe, low-traffic window** to switch back, to minimize disruption.
3. Verifying the repaired primary is genuinely healthy and fully caught up before cutting traffic back to it.

### Real-Life Analogy

If your car (primary) breaks down and you switch to your friend's spare car (standby/failover) to get to work all week, "failback" isn't just "drive your car again" the moment it's fixed — you'd first need to update your car's GPS/calendar with everything that happened during the week you were using the other car, and pick a sensible moment (like a weekend, not mid-commute) to switch back.

## 10.8 Backup Strategies — Tying Back to RPO/RTO

This section directly connects Module 9's toolkit to this module's RPO/RTO targets, which is exactly the kind of synthesis a senior interview question often demands:

| RPO/RTO Requirement | Appropriate Strategy (from Module 9 + this module) |
|---|---|
| RPO: 24h, RTO: hours | Nightly full backup, restore on demand |
| RPO: 1h, RTO: 1h | Hourly incremental backups + documented restore runbook |
| RPO: near-zero, RTO: minutes | Continuous log archiving/PITR + hot standby with fast automatic failover |
| RPO: zero, RTO: seconds | Synchronous multi-region replication + automated failover (highest cost, reserved for the most critical systems) |

## 10.9 Business Continuity

### What is Business Continuity?

**Business Continuity** is the broader organizational discipline of ensuring an entire business — not just the database, not just IT systems — can continue operating (or resume operating quickly) through and after a disruptive event. Disaster Recovery (this module) is typically considered a **subset** of Business Continuity Planning, focused specifically on IT/data systems.

### Why This Broader Context Matters

A database engineer's DR plan doesn't exist in isolation — it must align with the organization's broader business continuity needs: What's the financial cost of downtime per hour? What are contractual/regulatory obligations (e.g., banking regulations often mandate specific RPO/RTO ceilings)? What's the reputational cost? These business-level questions are precisely what should drive the RPO/RTO targets that then drive all the technical decisions covered throughout Module 9 and this module.

## 10.10 PostgreSQL vs MySQL (InnoDB) vs Oracle — DR Feature Terminology

| Concept | PostgreSQL | MySQL (InnoDB) | Oracle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standby replication | Streaming Replication + `pg_basebackup` | Replication (async/semi-sync) | Oracle Data Guard |
| Automatic failover tooling | Patroni, repmgr (third-party/ecosystem tools) | MySQL Group Replication, Orchestrator | Data Guard Fast-Start Failover |
| Geo-redundancy pattern | Cross-region streaming replication | Cross-region async replication | Data Guard with remote standby |
| PITR support | Native (Module 9) | Native via binlog replay | Native via Flashback + RMAN |

## 10.11 Advantages of Formal DR Planning

- Converts vague anxiety about "what if something terrible happens" into concrete, measurable, achievable targets (RPO/RTO) that engineering can actually design against.
- Provides a shared vocabulary between engineering and business stakeholders, so trade-off conversations ("do we really need zero data loss, given the cost?") happen deliberately rather than by accident.
- Regular DR drills surface real gaps in tooling, documentation, and team readiness *before* a real disaster forces the discovery at the worst possible time.

## 10.12 Disadvantages / Costs

- Achieving very low RPO/RTO targets (especially near-zero) is expensive — geo-redundant infrastructure, synchronous replication, and 24/7 on-call readiness all cost real money and engineering effort.
- DR planning and drilling consumes ongoing engineering time that produces no visible feature value — a genuine, recurring prioritization tension in most organizations.

## 10.13 Best Practices

- Set RPO/RTO targets **collaboratively with business stakeholders**, not unilaterally by engineering — these are business risk decisions with technical implementation costs.
- Test the *entire* DR plan regularly, not just the backup restore step — include the human/organizational process (who decides, who executes, how communication happens).
- Different systems within the same organization can and should have *different* RPO/RTO targets based on their actual criticality — not every dataset deserves the most expensive, zero-RPO treatment.

## 10.14 Common Mistakes

- Setting aggressive RPO/RTO targets on paper without actually building or funding the infrastructure needed to meet them — a DR plan that hasn't been resourced is a false sense of security.
- Never actually testing failover/failback procedures until a real disaster forces it — exactly the "unverified backup" mistake from Module 9, but at the organizational/architectural scale.
- Confusing HA with DR and assuming redundant servers within one data center are sufficient protection against a genuinely catastrophic, region-wide event.

## 10.15 Edge Cases

- **Split-brain during failover:** If a network partition (Module 2) makes the primary *appear* failed when it's actually still running and accepting writes, an automatic failover could create two simultaneously active "primaries" — a dangerous, data-corrupting scenario requiring careful consensus/quorum design, covered fully in Part 10.
- **Cascading regional failures:** Rare, but real — a disaster affecting one region can sometimes create unexpected load or cascading effects on the "safe" standby region (e.g., a massive traffic surge to the surviving region overwhelming its capacity) — a genuine, sophisticated DR planning consideration for very large-scale systems.

---

## 10.16 Chapter Summary

- **RPO** measures acceptable data loss (backward-looking from the failure moment); **RTO** measures acceptable downtime (forward-looking from the failure moment) — these are the two central metrics of any DR strategy.
- **Disaster Recovery Planning** formalizes targets, architecture, roles, and tested runbooks for responding to catastrophic failure.
- **High Availability** handles frequent, smaller-scale failures within a site; **Disaster Recovery** handles rare, large-scale, cross-site disasters — both are needed together.
- **Geo-Redundancy** physically separates data/infrastructure across regions to survive regional disasters, with synchronous vs asynchronous replication trading commit latency against RPO.
- **Failover** switches to a standby during a disaster; **Failback** is the deliberate, carefully re-synchronized process of switching back once the original primary is repaired.
- **Business Continuity** is the broader organizational umbrella that DR serves, ultimately driven by real business risk tolerance and cost considerations.

## 10.19 MCQs (with Answers)

**Q1.** A system with an RTO of 5 minutes most likely requires:
A) Manual restore from a nightly backup
B) Automated failover to a hot standby
C) No backup strategy at all
D) A weekly full backup only

**Answer: B**

**Q2.** High Availability primarily protects against:
A) Region-wide natural disasters
B) Frequent, smaller-scale, single-site failures
C) Human error only
D) Software licensing issues

**Answer: B**

**Q3.** Synchronous geo-replication trades:
A) Storage cost for RPO
B) Commit latency for near-zero RPO
C) CPU usage for RTO
D) Nothing — it has no trade-offs

**Answer: B**

**Q4.** Failback should occur:
A) Automatically and immediately once the primary is repaired
B) After re-synchronization and during a deliberately chosen safe window
C) Never — the standby should remain primary forever
D) Before the standby has processed any new transactions

**Answer: B**
